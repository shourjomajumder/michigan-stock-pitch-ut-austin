# Agent 03: Academic evidence on FFP reciprocity, codeshares and alliances (overlapping routes)

Search budget used: 5 of 5 WebSearch. Lederman 2007 (RAND) full text is paywalled; her MIT thesis PDF could not be fetched (403). No Lederman 2007 share magnitude was found in any public source, so none is reported.

## 1. Gayle and Brown, "Airline Strategic Alliances in Overlapping Markets: Should Policymakers be Concerned?" (Jan 2015 version, first version Jun 2008; Economics of Transportation)
URL: https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/62355/1/MPRA_paper_62355.pdf
- Delta/Continental/Northwest partners "leave their joint passenger share 1.9% higher in their codeshare markets" compared with non-codeshare markets.
- They also "leave their passenger traffic 1.8% lower in their codeshare markets" (share rose because rivals' traffic fell more).
- "the demand-increasing effect is only evident in markets that the partners have a substantial joint passenger share" (over 49% before the alliance, per paper).
- No estimate of switching between partners is given.
- Read for 10% case: a 1.9% gain in joint share is the alliance's whole effect. Switching inside the alliance (JetBlue to United) is not measured but would be a reallocation inside that pair. Points BELOW 10%.

## 2. Gayle, "An Empirical Analysis of the Competitive Effects of the Delta/Continental/Northwest Codeshare Alliance" (Oct 2007 version; Journal of Law and Economics)
URL: https://www.k-state.edu/economics/pdfs/websitefiles/gayle/dlconw-october-2007.pdf
- Alliance "accounts for 10.7% to 12.3% increase in overall city pair traffic" on alliance city pairs.
- "19.8% to 24.4% increase in the partners' city pair traffic on alliance city pairs."
- Only "21.9% of the alliance city pairs in the sample involves overlap."
- On overlap routes, "the alliance partners did not restrict traffic on alliance city pairs in which the partners' service overlap" (coefficient 0.0278, not significant). Overlap traffic for all carriers: -0.00097, not significant. Price effect of overlap: -0.0050, not significant.
- Traditional codeshare on overlap routes: partner traffic coefficient 0.1937 (5% level), all-carrier traffic 0.0536 (10% level).
- Cited in same paper: Bamberger, Carlton and Neumann (2004), domestic alliances "associated with a 7.5% and 3.9% fall in average fares".
- Read for 10% case: the +20% is joint growth for the partners, not one partner taking passengers from the other. The overlap routes show no significant reallocation. That says nothing direct about the split between partners and gives no support for large switching inside the alliance. Neutral to BELOW.

## 3. Lederman, "Are Frequent-Flyer Programs a Cause of the 'Hub Premium'?" (J. Economics & Management Strategy 17(1), 2008)
URL (abstract): https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jemstr:v:17:y:2008:i:1:p:35-66
- "I find that offering the FFP points of the dominant carrier at an airport does, indeed, lead to higher fares."
- "FFPs may account for at least 25% of the 'hub premium.'"
- U of T Magazine summary (Ken McGuffin, 25 Jun 2008, https://magazine.utoronto.ca/?p=2445): "frequent flyer programs driving airfares at key hub airports up by about five per cent"; premium tickets "pushed even higher – by about nine per cent"; "about $7 to $10" a one-way flight out of a hub; "at least $25 for a one-way business ticket". Example: United fares out of Atlanta rose after the Delta partnership.
- Mechanism match: when a partner can earn the dominant carrier's points, it gains enough loyalty value to charge about 5% more at that carrier's airport. For JetBlue, that means United at JetBlue-heavy BOS/JFK. The study shows the partner gaining pricing power (it can charge more), not passengers leaving the dominant carrier. If United takes the gain as higher fares instead of more passengers, that points to modest switching. Bounds the effect: modest, BELOW 10%.

## 4. Lederman PhD thesis, MIT 2003 (abstract only)
URL: https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/17626
- Chapter 1: FFP enhancements through international partnerships raise market share, "larger at airports where the airline is more dominant" (paraphrase of abstract).
- Chapter 2: the 1998 domestic FFP partnerships "were associated with market share gains, though they may also make the partners' flights more substitutable on overlapping routes" (fetch summary of abstract; no magnitudes).
- Read for 10% case: direct qualitative support for the switching idea (more substitutable on overlapping routes), but no number.

## 5. Bar, Chernomaz and Escobari, "Pricing and Travelers' Decision To Use Frequent Flyer Miles" (May 2010, MPRA 32201)
URL: https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/32201/1/MPRA_paper_32201.pdf
- "on average airlines have 4.85% of the travelers using their accumulated miles to obtain a ticket."
- A 10-pt rise in a carrier's departure share raises its award-ticket share by 0.24 pp.
- Read for 10% case: this is context. Award travel is about 5% of passengers, and the burn side of reciprocity acts on a small group.

## Others checked (no usable magnitudes)
- Armantier and Richard, "Domestic airline alliances and consumer welfare" (RAND 2008): Continental/Northwest codeshare had no significant welfare effect overall; no share numbers in abstract.
- Escobari (2010, MPRA 36231): 1% rise in FFP traveler share is associated with 1.16% higher fares; repeats Lederman's 25%.
- Bilotkach and Pai (2009 slides): qualitative only ("FFP partnerships help non-dominant carriers get dominance premium").

## Time series
None found. These are panel and difference-in-differences studies that give point estimates, not month or quarter paths.

## Overall
The academic literature finds that alliance and FFP-reciprocity effects on share are small: about 2% joint share (Gayle and Brown), about 5% fare premium for the non-dominant partner (Lederman 2008), and no significant reallocation on overlap routes (Gayle 2007). It does say qualitatively that reciprocity makes partners more substitutable on overlap routes, but none of these studies puts a number on switching from one partner to the other. Taken together, they suggest a base case at the low end of 2-20% (about 2-5%) and give no support for 10%. One caveat: the 1998-2008 alliances had no product-quality gap like Starlink or the on-time difference.
